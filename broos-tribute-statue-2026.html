Broos Deserves a Statue — Sports Minister McKenzie Leads Tribute to Departing Coach

A look back at the five-year transformation of Bafana Bafana under Hugo Broos, as tributes pour in from across South African football.

📅 10 July 2026 ✍️ Matchday Soccer Planet Editorial 🏷️ Bafana Bafana, Tribute ⏱️ 4 min read

Sports Minister Gayton McKenzie did not hold back when asked about Hugo Broos's five-year tenure as Bafana Bafana head coach. "He deserves a statue," McKenzie said, as tributes poured in for the departing coach following South Africa's historic 2026 World Cup campaign — a rare moment of unqualified praise for a South African football administration more often defined by its criticisms.

From Outside the Top 70 to World Cup History

When Broos arrived in May 2021, Bafana Bafana were ranked outside the top 70 in the world and were, in his own later words, something of a punchline in continental football conversations. Five years later, they have reached a World Cup knockout stage for the first time in the country's history, won bronze at AFCON 2024, and produced a generation of players — Mofokeng, Maseko, Mokoena, Mudau — now attracting serious attention from clubs across Europe.

A Simple, Effective Philosophy

Broos's methods were, by his own description, simple but effective: select players on current form rather than reputation, build a defensive identity the team could rely on under pressure, and trust youth over experience when the data supported it. He once famously dropped veteran players who had seemed untouchable for years — a decision that caused genuine outrage among sections of the fanbase at the time, before being emphatically proved right by results.

"What the coach has done for this country, no one can take away." — Ronwen Williams, Bafana Bafana captain, speaking after the World Cup

The Numbers Behind Five Historic Years

Broos leaves as the longest-serving Bafana Bafana head coach since the country's readmission to international football in 1992, with a record of 26 wins, 22 draws and 12 losses from 60 matches in charge. Those numbers alone would represent a solid tenure by any standard, but it is the shape of the improvement — from outside the world's top 70 to a maiden World Cup knockout appearance — that has made his spell feel genuinely transformative rather than merely steady. Under his guidance, South Africa qualified for a FIFA World Cup for the first time since co-hosting the 2010 tournament, then went a step further than qualification alone by winning a knockout match once they arrived in North America.

A Nation Reflects on a Coaching Philosophy

Part of what has made the tributes feel so widely shared is that Broos's approach never relied on marquee reputations. Squad selections were driven by current form and tactical fit rather than name recognition, which meant a changing cast of players moved in and out of his matchday plans depending on who was playing well at any given moment. It is an approach that occasionally frustrated fans attached to established favourites, but one that ultimately produced the deepest pool of internationally attractive young talent South African football has had in a generation — from Relebohile Mofokeng's move to Belgium to Thapelo Maseko's World Cup heroics.

What Comes Next

Broos is currently in Belgium and plans to return to South Africa at the end of July to say his goodbyes in person. He has indicated to SAFA president Danny Jordaan that he remains open to an advisory role with the federation, even as his coaching career draws to a close. Whether that advisory role materialises, and who ultimately succeeds him on the touchline, will shape the next chapter of Bafana Bafana as the team looks to build on the momentum of a genuinely historic campaign. SAFA's search for a successor is already well underway, with several high-profile South African coaching names in the conversation.

The Players Who Carry His Fingerprints

Perhaps the clearest measure of Broos's legacy is the sheer number of players who moved abroad or elevated their standing during his tenure. Relebohile Mofokeng's reported R70 million move to Royale Union Saint-Gilloise came directly off the back of a World Cup in which Broos trusted him with a starting role. Thapelo Maseko, Teboho Mokoena and Khuliso Mudau all enhanced their reputations under his management, while a core of Mamelodi Sundowns and Orlando Pirates players benefited from the tactical discipline and match sharpness his coaching demanded. Few Bafana Bafana squads in recent memory have produced this concentration of players attracting genuine interest from clubs outside South Africa's borders.

An Emotional Farewell Still to Come

For all the statistics and tactical analysis, Broos's exit will ultimately be remembered as an emotional one. His planned return to South Africa at the end of July, specifically to say goodbye in person rather than departing quietly from Belgium, speaks to how seriously he has taken his relationship with the country and its football community over five years. For a coach who arrived as a relative outsider tasked with rebuilding a team ranked outside the world's top 70, choosing to close that chapter face to face with players, staff and supporters says as much about his five years in charge as any result on the pitch.
